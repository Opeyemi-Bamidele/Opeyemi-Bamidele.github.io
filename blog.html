Why a feature ranking is not enough to drop a test

A strengthened beam is only as trustworthy as the tests behind its assessment. Here is how I asked which tests could safely be skipped, and how the answer was checked.

Read the note

The cost of knowing

The built environment accounts for about 40% of global energy use and solid waste. Retrofitting existing structures to extend their service life is one of the most direct ways to reduce that footprint, and strengthening reinforced concrete beams with fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP), a high-strength composite bonded to the beam, is a widely used way to do it.

Strengthening raises its own question: will this particular beam perform safely? Answering it has traditionally depended on extensive laboratory testing of geometric and material properties, a process that is costly, slow and resource-intensive. The study asked whether a reliable assessment is possible from a reduced set of parameters, and therefore with less testing.

Two predictions from one dataset

Working from an experimental database of 624 FRP-strengthened RC beams, I built models that predict two things together: the governing failure mode, meaning the way the beam finally fails, which decides which design checks matter, and the ultimate load capacity. Under a random specimen-level evaluation, the classifier reached a Macro-F1 of 0.93 and an MCC of 0.91, and the load model reached R² = 0.96 with an RMSE of 9.59 kN.

Beams from the same source study tend to resemble each other, which can flatter a model. To check generalisation, the work added grouped cross-validation and leave-one-study-out cross-validation, where entire independent studies are held back.

Removing inputs with evidence

The central contribution is a staged feature-elimination pipeline. Boruta screening compares each real input with shuffled copies of itself and keeps only the ones that beat the noise. Cross-validated SHAP and permutation importance then give two different views of how much each remaining input drives the predictions. Finally, a factorial ablation retrains the model with inputs removed in combinations and tests whether performance actually changes.

A ranking alone is not enough for this decision. It tells you which inputs a model leans on, not whether the model gets worse without them. And comparing scores from repeated cross-validation needs care: the folds share training data, so they are not independent, and an ordinary test would overstate significance. With many comparisons, the chance of a false positive also grows. The paired significance tests were corrected for both problems.

What it means for an engineer

Two inputs came out as removable, including FRP tensile strength, which can only be determined by destructive coupon testing. Removing them takes the input set from 12 to 10 with no statistically significant loss of predictive performance on the holdout test set. The conclusion held across alternative model families and was supported by bootstrap confidence intervals, and training time fell by about 15%.

The result is a validated minimum viable assessment parameter set: one fewer destructive test before a retrofit decision can be made. The work lines up, in qualitative terms, with SDG 9 (industry, innovation and infrastructure), SDG 12 (responsible consumption and production) and SDG 13 (climate action). What I value most is not the accuracy figure but the argument: a smaller test programme backed by statistics rather than by intuition.

Turning climate data into a design gap

Lagos buildings are designed against 1990s data. Thirty-five years of records show how far that has drifted, and what it means for walls, cooling plant and façades.

A code written for another climate

Buildings in Lagos need mechanical cooling all year, and air conditioning accounts for up to 80% of residential electricity consumption. Yet the Nigerian Building Code (NB 485:2017) still refers to thermal design data from the 1990s. Background warming and an intensifying urban heat island have widened the gap between those assumptions and real conditions. The paper started from the fact that no study had linked long-term cooling-demand trends to quantified engineering design shortfalls for any Nigerian city.

Measuring cooling demand

A cooling degree day measures how far, and for how long, the outdoor temperature rises above a base temperature at which a building starts to need cooling. Adding the daily values gives a measure of cooling demand over a period. I calculated them from 12,784 daily temperature records for 1990 to 2024, at four engineering base temperatures: 22 °C, 23.3 °C, 26 °C and 28 °C, with 23.3 °C as the primary design reference.

Detecting a trend honestly

The Mann–Kendall test looks for a consistent upward or downward trend without assuming the data follow a normal distribution. Climate series have memory, though: a hot year tends to follow a hot year, which makes simple tests overconfident. So the analysis used trend-free pre-whitening to remove that persistence before testing. Sen’s slope then measures the size of the trend as the median slope between all pairs of points, which keeps a few extreme years from distorting it.

Significantly increasing trends were confirmed at three of the four base temperatures. At the primary 23.3 °C reference the Sen’s slope is +4.55 °C·days per year (p < 0.01). A structural break analysis identifies 2015 as the start of a persistently above-baseline thermal regime, and in the most recent sub-period mean cooling degree days exceed the 1990–2001 design baseline by up to 50% at the higher base temperatures.

From statistic to design gap

A trend does not change a design on its own, so the step that mattered was translating it into quantities a designer uses. The first is the envelope U-value trajectory: how well insulated the walls would have to be as the climate warms. The second is an HVAC capacity undersizing index, which compares installed cooling capacity with what demand now requires. The third is the annual count of thermal cycles, used as a proxy for fatigue loading, since daily expansion and contraction of a façade accumulates damage over time.

The results were clear. The dominant uninsulated sandcrete wall typology fails the ASHRAE 90.1-2019 Zone 1A prescriptive limits throughout the study period, installed HVAC systems are already operating in the engineering caution zone, and façade fatigue loading has intensified markedly since 2015.

What I take from it

Climate data becomes useful to structural and building engineers when it is expressed as a design gap with a number attached. The same framework is replicable for climate-adaptive code revision in similar hot–humid climates across sub-Saharan Africa.

Can waste and ash replace part of the cement under a road?

Three studies on stabilising lateritic subgrade with kaolin, rice husk ash and electronic waste, what each test measures, and what the results showed.

Read the note
3published papers
16kaolin–NaOH treatment mixes
15% + 12%rice husk ash + sodium carbonate, best blend
10% + 6%cement + e-waste for required strength

The problem under the road

A road pavement rests on a foundation layer called the subgrade. Across tropical Nigeria that is usually lateritic soil, which is often highly plastic, sensitive to moisture and low in strength: the natural soil in the e-waste study had a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) below 5%. Cement and lime are the conventional stabilisers, but their production carries high carbon emissions. At the same time, mismanaged electronic waste is a serious environmental threat in Nigeria, even though the sector holds real economic value. Our series of studies asked whether waste and alkali-activated materials could do part of the job.

How the soil was judged

Each study characterised the soil and additives chemically and mineralogically, using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) for oxide composition and X-ray diffraction (XRD) for the minerals present. Treated soil was then tested for Atterberg limits, which show how plastic it is, for compaction, which shows how densely it packs, and for CBR, the standard measure of subgrade strength used in pavement design. The rice husk ash study added soaked and unsoaked CBR, unconfined compressive strength and shear strength tests, following British Standards.

Three combinations, three lessons

Kaolin with sodium hydroxide. Kaolin (0, 7.5, 15 and 22.5%) and sodium hydroxide (0, 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5%) were combined in 16 treatment mixes. XRF and XRD confirmed that both the soil and the kaolin are aluminosilicate materials. Either additive alone gave inconsistent CBR compared with untreated soil, but the combined treatment improved workability at 15% kaolin with 1.5% sodium hydroxide, and CBR at 7.5% kaolin with 1.0% sodium hydroxide. The lesson is that the two additives work as a system, not as separate ingredients.

Rice husk ash with sodium carbonate. Ash (0–15%) and sodium carbonate (0–12%) were tested together. The ash is rich in reactive silica and the soil is predominantly kaolinitic, which sets up an alkali-activated pozzolanic reaction: the alkali helps dissolve silica and alumina so that they can react and bind the soil particles. Stabilisation significantly reduced plasticity and raised strength, and the blend of 15% ash with 12% sodium carbonate gave the highest CBR, UCS and shear strength. A two-way ANOVA confirmed that both stabilisers had a significant influence on the key properties.

Crumbed e-waste with cement. E-waste (0–9%) and cement (0–15%) were added to a clayey soil of moderate plasticity. The e-waste, at 6%, increased interparticle friction and stiffness, while the cement improved bonding within the soil–e-waste matrix. The required strength was reached with 10% cement and 6% e-waste, which points to a more sustainable way of stabilising lateritic soil while giving crumbed e-waste a productive use.

My part

My contribution to this series was the statistical analysis and manuscript drafting. It taught me to treat a test matrix as a statistical design from the start, so that the analysis can say which factors matter and whether they work together.

Telling damage apart from the weather

Bridges change with temperature and load. A monitoring method has to separate that from real damage, and I compared four ways of doing it, including how each one fails.

Read the note
216samples over 9 days
4strategies compared
24 / 24damage cases detected
3 / 48false alarms, best method

The problem

Structural damage lowers a structure’s natural vibration frequencies, but so does ordinary temperature drift. A monitoring method that cannot separate the two will either miss damage or raise alarms that nobody trusts. The real challenge is telling them apart.

The data

The project used real modal-frequency data from the Yonghe cable-stayed bridge (Sarmadi & Daneshvar, 2022, Mendeley Data): four modal frequencies identified through Frequency Domain Decomposition, in 216 samples across nine days. Days 1 to 8 are healthy (192 samples) and day 9 is damaged (24 samples).

Why Mahalanobis distance

Modes 1 and 2 are strongly correlated across the healthy samples (r = 0.89), because both are driven by the same underlying factor, the overall stiffness of the structure, which is itself governed by temperature. A threshold set on each mode separately ignores that relationship. The Mahalanobis distance encodes it, so it can flag a sample that breaks the normal pattern between the modes even when neither value looks extreme on its own.

Four strategies, four failure modes

  1. Fixed baseline. The mean and covariance come from 144 training samples (75% of the healthy data), and new samples are flagged against a 99% chi-square control limit. It detected 24 of 24 damage cases with 6 of 48 false alarms, but it degrades over time, because the raw frequencies drift steadily downward through the healthy period and a single baseline cannot tell that drift from damage.
  2. Naive rolling window. The baseline is recomputed from the 20 samples just before each point, so that “normal” can adapt. It produced 7 of 48 false alarms but detected only 3 of 24 damage cases: within one or two samples of damage starting, the window absorbs the damaged readings into its own baseline, the distance spikes and then collapses, and the detector forgets anything is wrong.
  3. Clean-buffer rolling window. This fixes the contamination problem by updating the buffer only with samples not already flagged. It detected 24 of 24 damage cases but gave 21 of 48 false alarms, the worst of all: once a sample is falsely flagged, the buffer freezes and goes stale, making later samples look even more anomalous and starting a cascade.
  4. Linear detrending with a fixed baseline. A linear trend is fitted for each mode on the training data only and subtracted from the full dataset, then the fixed-baseline method runs on what is left. This was the best overall: 24 of 24 damage cases detected and 3 of 48 false alarms.

The fitted slopes are physically interpretable. Modes 1, 2 and 4 have clear negative slopes, which is temperature-driven softening, while Mode 3 is nearly flat, consistent with Mode 3 having the weakest covariance with the other three modes.

What I took from it

Each method’s failure was diagnosed rather than just observed, and the fix was traced back to its exact cause. That habit of asking why a method breaks sits behind two companion projects as well: a finite-difference model of wave reflection at damage, and a machine-learning surrogate for offshore monopile fatigue. All three are on the Research page, with code on GitHub.
