Resource-Efficient Machine Learning via Multi-Staged Feature Elimination for Sustainable Structural Assessment and Life-Extension of FRP-Strengthened RC Beams

Bamidele, O., Adisa, J., Labar, B., & Bektaş, N.

Abstract

Retrofitting existing structures to extend service life is vital for reducing the built environment's massive footprint, which accounts for 40% of global energy use and solid waste. Fiber-Reinforced Polymer (FRP) strengthening is a widely used retrofit technique for reinforced concrete (RC) beams. However, assessing whether a strengthened beam will perform safely traditionally depends on extensive laboratory testing of geometric and material properties, a process that is costly, slow, and resource-intensive. This study evaluates whether reliable structural assessment can be achieved with a reduced parameter set, thereby minimizing the testing burden. Using an experimental database of 624 FRP-strengthened RC beam specimens, we developed machine learning models that jointly predict governing failure mode and ultimate load capacity, achieving Macro-F1 = 0.93 and MCC = 0.91 for classification, and R² = 0.96 (RMSE = 9.59 kN) for capacity prediction under a random specimen-level evaluation protocol, with additional grouped and leave-one-study-out cross-validation performed to assess generalization across independent source studies. Beyond predictive accuracy, this study's central contribution is a staged feature-elimination pipeline combining Boruta screening, cross-validated SHAP and permutation importance ranking, and factorial ablation with paired statistical significance testing corrected for the non-independence of repeated cross-validation folds and for multiple comparisons. Results demonstrate that two input variables, including FRP tensile strength, whose determination requires destructive coupon testing, can be eliminated with no statistically significant loss in predictive performance on the holdout test set, a conclusion further supported by robustness checks across alternative model families and by uncertainty quantification via bootstrap confidence intervals. This reduces the required input set from 12 to 10, establishing a validated minimum viable assessment parameter set that meaningfully cuts the testing, time, and resource burden of retrofit decision-making, while also lowering model training time by approximately 15%, in qualitative alignment with SDG 9, SDG 12, and SDG 13.

Effects of Kaolin and Sodium Hydroxide on the Geotechnical Properties of Lateritic Soil

Oluremi, J. R., Yusuf, A. L., Bamidele, O. A., & Osuolale, O. M.

Abstract

Lateritic soils in tropical regions often exhibit high plasticity and moisture sensitivity that compromise pavement performance, even where bearing capacity is otherwise adequate. This study investigated the stabilization of lateritic soil using varying proportions of kaolin (0, 7.5, 15 and 22.5%) and sodium hydroxide, NaOH (0, 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5%) across 16 treatment combinations. Material characterization by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed both soil and kaolin as aluminosilicate materials, Atterberg limits, compaction, and California Bearing Ratio (CBR) tests, were used to evaluate the performance of the stabilized lateritic soil. The use of Kaolin or NaOH alone produced inconsistent CBR relative to untreated soil, but combined treatment led to improved workability and CBR of the stabilized material at 15% kaolin with 1.5% NaOH, and 7.5% kaolin with 1.0% NaOH, respectively. The admixture of kaolin-NaOH is proved to be a potential stabilizing system for lateritic subgrade improvement, but warrant further validation through replicated trials and comparison with conventional stabilizers.

Cooling Degree Day Trends and Their Implications for Building Thermal Design and Thermal Fatigue Loading in Lagos, Nigeria

Bamidele, O., Adisa, J., Labar, B., & Bektaş, N.

Abstract

Buildings in Lagos require mechanical cooling year-round, with air conditioning accounting for up to 80% of residential electricity consumption. Despite this, the Nigerian Building Code (NB 485:2017) still references 1990s thermal design data, creating a growing mismatch between design assumptions and actual thermal conditions. Compounding background warming and an intensifying urban heat island have widened this gap considerably, yet no study has linked long-term cooling demand trends to quantified engineering design shortfalls for any Nigerian city. This study presents a 35-year cooling degree day (CDD) trend analysis for Lagos (1990–2024), derived from 12,784 daily temperature records at four engineering base temperatures (22 °C, 23.3 °C, 26 °C, and 28 °C) respectively. Trends are detected using the Mann–Kendall test with Trend-Free Pre-Whitening and Sen’s slope as the magnitude estimator. Significantly increasing CDD trends are confirmed at three base temperatures, with a Sen’s slope of +4.55 °C·days yr⁻¹ at the primary design reference of 23.3 °C (p < 0.01). Structural break analysis identifies 2015 as the transition into a persistently above-baseline thermal regime, with mean CDD in the most recent sub-period exceeding the 1990–2001 design baseline by up to 50% at higher base temperatures. The detected trends are translated into three engineering gap analyses: required envelope U-value trajectories, an HVAC capacity undersizing index, and annual thermal cycling frequency as a structural fatigue proxy. Results show that the dominant uninsulated sandcrete typology fails ASHRAE 90.1-2019 Zone 1A prescriptive limits throughout the study horizon, installed HVAC systems are already operating in the engineering caution zone, and façade fatigue loading has intensified markedly since 2015. To the author’s knowledge, this study is the first to couple a statistically robust long-period CDD record for Lagos with code-referenced design gap figures, providing a replicable framework for climate-adaptive building code revision across similar hot–humid climates in sub-Saharan Africa.

Effect of Sodium Carbonate on Rice Husk Ash to Stabilize Lateritic Soil for Use in Road Construction

Oluremi, J. R., Oyegbami, A. P., Ayanwuyi, S. A., & Bamidele, O. A.

Abstract

Lateritic soils commonly used in tropical road construction often exhibit high plasticity and inadequate strength, limiting their direct application in pavement layers. This study investigates the effectiveness of rice husk ash (RHA) activated with sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) as a sustainable stabilizing system for lateritic soil improvement. The materials were characterized for their chemical and mineralogical composition using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The soil was treated with varying proportions of RHA (0–15%) and Na₂CO₃ (0–12%), and evaluated through Atterberg limits, compaction characteristics, California Bearing Ratio (CBR) under soaked and unsoaked conditions, unconfined compressive strength (UCS), and shear strength tests following relevant British Standards. A two-way ANOVA was employed to assess the statistical significance of the stabilizers’ effects. Results revealed that RHA is rich in reactive silica, while the lateritic soil is predominantly kaolinitic, enabling effective alkali-activated pozzolanic reactions. Stabilization significantly reduced plasticity and enhanced strength properties, with the optimum blend of 15% RHA and 12% Na₂CO₃ yielding the highest CBR, UCS, and shear strength values. Statistical analysis confirmed the significant influence of the stabilizers on key engineering properties. The study demonstrates that sodium carbonate–activated RHA is an effective and environmentally sustainable alternative for lateritic soil stabilization in pavement applications.

Compaction and Strength Characteristics of Cement Stabilized Lateritic Soil Admixed with Crumbed Electronic Waste for Road Construction

Alasinrin, S., Oluremi, J. R., Bamidele, O. A., & Abiola, O. A.

Abstract

Road pavement is built on lateritic soil as foundation or subgrade course of the road but it’s often characterized with low strength, and poor durability. Cement and lime have been found to be effective as conventional stabilizers for lateritic soil; however, their productions have been attributed to the high carbon emissions. At the same time, the mismanagement and unsafe recycling of electronic waste pose a major environmental threat in Nigeria, even though the sector holds considerable economic value. This study therefore assesses the stabilizing effect of crumbed e-waste and cement on lateritic soil to create a sustainable, high-performance road pavement material. Laboratory tests were conducted on soil samples treated with varying e-waste (0–9%) and cement (0–15%) contents. The soil was characterized for particle size, Atterberg limits, and compaction, while stabilized samples were tested for CBR.The natural soil was clayey with moderate plasticity, and poor bearing capacity (CBR< 5%). The presence of e-waste content (6%) enhanced interparticle friction and stiffness while cement improved the bonding properties within the matrix of mixture of lateritic soil and e-waste thereby enhancing its strength. The required strength performance of the mixture was achieved with 10% cement and 6% e-waste for sustainable approach to lateritic soil stabilization.

Time-dependent structural degradation of concrete in leachate-contaminated soils: Experimental and mathematical modelling approaches

Adewuyi, A. P., Ayanlere, S. A., Adedokun, S. I., Kareem, M. A., Bamidele, O. A., Adisa, J. O., Akingbade, J. O., & Akintayo, Z. O.

Abstract

This study investigates the time-dependent degradation of concrete exposed to leachate-contaminated soils, focusing on two dumpsites: Atenda and Alakasu, within the Ogbomoso metropolis. Concrete cubes (150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm) were cast, cured, and buried at a depth of 1 m in both contaminated and natural soils for periods of 28, 60, 90, and 150 days. The compressive strength of the specimens was evaluated, and empirical models were fitted to describe the strength–time trend within the tested period. Results show a marked strength reduction in specimens from the Atenda site, with compressive strength decreasing from 18.34 N/mm² at 28 days to 14.50 N/mm² at 150 days, corresponding to a 21% loss. Conversely, specimens from the Alakasu site exhibited marginal strength gains, indicating a less aggressive environment. The mathematical modeling revealed a logarithmic degradation trend for Atenda (R² = 0.96) and a polynomial pattern for Alakasu (R² = 0.98), indicating different degradation behaviour at the two sites. These findings suggest that site conditions, possibly including leachate chemistry, influence the durability of buried concrete elements. Soil and leachate chemistry were not measured in this study and provide a basis for further investigation of service-life behaviour in similar geo-environmental settings.

The Preparedness Paradox and Transnational Lifelines following the 2025 Afghanistan Earthquake

Bektaş, N., Bamidele, O., Sioufi, M., Khalfallah, D., Ansari, A., Alkozai, W., Labar, B., Salman, F. N., Farkas, D., & Naseri, J.

Abstract

Natural hazard-induced disasters constitute a profound impediment to sustainable socioeconomic development, particularly in developing nations where systemic vulnerabilities exacerbate human and economic losses. This research examines the magnitude 6.0 earthquake that struck eastern Afghanistan on August 31, 2025, presenting a critical case for analyzing the intersection of high-intensity hazards, cascading infrastructure failure, and social fragility in conflict-affected contexts. Originating at a shallow focal depth of approximately 8 km near Jalalabad, the event resulted in the region's deadliest seismic disaster since 1998, with widespread destruction, nearly 2,000 confirmed fatalities, and direct economic losses estimated at US$183 million. To systematically evaluate disaster response and resilience dynamics, this study deployed a multi-language, dual-track survey capturing empirical data from 189 respondents across 15 countries, encompassing both in-country survivors and the global Afghan diaspora. The analysis reveals three fundamental socio-technical dynamics that dictated crisis outcomes. First, an Awareness-Preparedness Paradox was observed: despite high risk awareness, 77.8% of in-country households lacked emergency plans and 66.7% lacked survival kits prior to the event, demonstrating how acute economic exhaustion prevents investment in proactive risk mitigation. Second, cascading infrastructure and telecommunication failures created severe "last-mile" aid gaps, resulting in 66.7% of ground-level survivors receiving no formal institutional aid and forcing a heavy reliance on localized social capital and mutual aid networks. Third, the study identifies a Sympathy-Action Gap among international observers—whose high perceived severity did not translate to donations due to low institutional trust—contrasted by the rapid mobilization of the diaspora, serving as a vital transnational lifeline for informal financial aid. Despite these systemic challenges, NLP sentiment and topic analysis (n = 123) indicates strong latent community agency: qualitative responses divide roughly evenly between calls for institutionalized disaster education and preparedness (56.7%) and expressions of immediate material relief needs (43.3%), with survivors in both categories positioning themselves as active agents rather than passive aid recipients. These findings underscore the urgent need to transition from reactive relief strategies toward proactive, community-based disaster risk management (CBDRM) to operationalize the priorities of the Sendai Framework and "Build Back Better" in highly seismically active regions.
